Summary

Figure 1. Given 8 training views, our method maintains low Average Error across interpolated novel views, whereas the baseline exhibits overfitting. Plots are means and standard deviations over 10 runs.


3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) [1] lacks generalization across novel viewpoints in a few-shot scenario because it overfits to the sparse observations (Fig. 1). We reformulate the 3DGS training objective, thereby explicitly guiding 3DGS to converge toward better generalization.

Motivation

Figure 2. Conceptual 1D Loss Landscape of Flat and Sharp Minima [2]. Flat minimum better generalize then sharp minimum.


Improving generalization—the ability of a model to perform accurately on unseen data—has been a long-standing problem of interest in deep learning. Keskar et al. [2] show that sharp minima exhibit a large gap between train and test loss, whereas flat minima show a much smaller gap (Fig. 2). This tendency motivated research to investigate the relationship between generalization and loss landscape. Consequently, Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM) [3] jointly minimizes both empirical loss and sharpness, which is equivalent to minimizing the worst-case loss within a neighborhood of radius ρ.

The model parameters w are updated with the gradient computed at the estimated local maximum w+ε. In practice, the local maximum is approximated via first-order Taylor expansion for the loss function around the current parameters, perturbing the parameters in the gradient direction with magnitude ρ.

The follow-up work WSAM [4] revisits sharpness as a regularization term by weighting the estimated sharpness. When the weight hyperparameter γ is set to 0, the sharpness term vanishes and the optimization minimizes the empirical loss only. When γ = 0.5, WSAM is equivalent to SAM, while for 0.5 < γ < 1, the sharpness term is emphasized.

Hypothesis

Figure 3. High-frequency regions requires sharp minima for accurate reconstruction, while low-frequency regions favor flat minima.


However, directly applying SAM to 3DGS is suboptimal. The importance of sharpness for accurate reconstruction actually varies across pixels (Fig. 3), and since SAM regularize sharpness uniformly, it produces blurry results (Fig. 4).

Figure 4. Our method preserves fine details that SAM tends to oversmooth.


Based on this intuition, we hypothesize that for each Gaussian, the optimal perturbation magnitude ρ and regularization weight γ vary in correlation with image frequency.

Method

Figure 5. Method overview.


Before applying frequency adaptivity, we begin by perturbing the per-Gaussian attribute. Going a step further, we adapt the perturbation magnitude according to the local image frequency (Fig. 5). Finally, we adapt the regularization weight according to the frequency of each Gaussian (Fig. 6).
